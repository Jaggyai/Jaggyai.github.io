You are not imagining it, and you are not failing at it

You gained weight around your middle without changing a thing. You wake at 3 a.m. You lost a word mid-sentence in a meeting. You went to your doctor, described four of it, and left with a prescription for something else and the feeling you’d wasted the appointment.

That is not a personal failure. Ninety-five percent of Canadian women get symptoms, averaging seven of more than thirty — and most of those thirty are never mentioned to them. Fifty-eight percent didn’t know that migraines, anxiety, low mood and memory problems were even on the list.

The gap isn’t information. There’s plenty of that. The gap is that almost none of it is Canadian — it doesn’t know what your province covers, how to get through a Canadian appointment, or what you can claim through your benefits.

Questions people ask

Is Carol a doctor?

No, and the course never pretends otherwise. Carol went through this herself and spent years figuring out how the Canadian system works. The Playbook is education and navigation — it makes you a better-prepared patient. Every module ends the same way: take this to your doctor.

Will this tell me whether to take hormone therapy?

No. That decision is between you and your prescriber, and it depends on your history. What the Playbook does is explain the options in plain English, tell you what your province covers, and give you the questions to ask so the conversation is a real one.

I’m 43 and not sure I’m in perimenopause yet.

That’s the most common place to start, and it’s the best time to do this. Perimenopause typically begins years before periods stop. Take the quiz first — it’s free, and it will tell you whether this is worth your time right now.

Is there a meal plan?

Deliberately not. Individual nutrition prescriptions need a Registered Dietitian, and anyone selling you one without that credential is doing something they shouldn’t. Module 04 teaches the principles and shows you how to build a plate — which is more useful anyway, because it survives past week three.

Can I claim this through my benefits?

Often, yes — many Wellness and Lifestyle Spending Accounts cover health education. Your employer sets the eligible categories, so check with HR. We provide an itemised receipt either way.

What if it isn’t for me?

Thirty days, full refund, no explanation needed. If it didn’t help, it shouldn’t cost you.

What menopause care costs in Canada

Coverage is not the same across the country, and almost nobody explains the difference. Here’s the short version — the full cheat sheet, one page per province, comes with the Playbook.

WhatWho paysWhat to know
Menopausal hormone therapy Public — in some provinces In BC, MHT is covered at $0 under PharmaCare’s national pharmacare plan as of March 1, 2026 — no medication cost, no dispensing fee, no special authority needed. Manitoba has a comparable program. Elsewhere, coverage depends on your provincial formulary and your extended health plan.
Non-hormonal prescriptions Mixed Varies by province and by drug. Some newer options are covered only through private plans. Ask your pharmacist to check both your provincial plan and your private plan before you fill.
Your GP appointment Provincial health plan Covered — but capped in practice by appointment length. This is why preparation matters more than anything else on this page.
Dietitian, physio, psychology Usually private Rarely covered publicly for menopause. Most people access these through an employer’s extended health plan or a Health Spending Account.
Coaching, courses, gym, wellness Wellness / Lifestyle Spending Account Employers set their own eligible categories, and fitness and wellness education commonly qualify. Amounts vary — often several hundred dollars a year. Note that WSA amounts are treated as taxable income.

Coverage details change. Confirm current coverage with your provincial program or your pharmacist before making decisions. Last reviewed September 2026.

The part most people miss

Free hormone therapy is a real win, and it fixes a real set of symptoms. What it does not do is address the things most women say bother them most — the change in body composition, the loss of strength, the fatigue, the sleep. Those need different work, and no provincial plan covers it.

That’s the gap the Playbook was written for.

Who’s behind this

Carol Danis

I’m not a doctor. I’m the person who spent two years being told she was fine.

It started with sleep. Then the weight that wouldn’t move no matter how carefully I ate. Then a meeting where I lost the word I was reaching for and had to sit there while someone finished my sentence. I went to my doctor four times over two years and left every appointment with the sense that I’d described it badly.

The fifth time, I brought a written list. That appointment changed everything — not because the doctor was different, but because I was prepared.

What I learned afterwards was that almost none of the good information out there is Canadian. It doesn’t know what your province covers. It doesn’t know how a walk-in works. It tells you to “ask your provider about testing” as if that were a straightforward request in an eight-minute appointment.

So I built the thing I needed. The Playbook is everything I figured out the hard way, in order, with the printable pages I wish someone had handed me at 46 — and it is honest about what it is. I’m not treating anyone. I’m getting you ready for the person who can.
